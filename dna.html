DNA

What is DNA?

DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) is the hereditary molecule that stores and transmits genetic information in nearly all living organisms. It contains the biological instructions that cells use to grow, function, repair themselves, and reproduce. These instructions are organized into genes, which are specific segments of DNA that provide the information needed to produce proteins or functional RNA molecules.

DNA is often described as the body's genetic blueprint because it carries the complete set of instructions required to build and maintain an organism. Every time a cell divides, its DNA is copied so that each new cell receives nearly the same genetic information.

In humans, nearly every cell contains DNA. Most of this DNA is located inside the nucleus, where it is packaged into structures called chromosomes. A small amount of DNA is also found in the mitochondria, the energy-producing organelles within cells. This mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) is inherited almost exclusively from a person's mother.

Structure

DNA has a unique structure known as a double helix, which resembles a twisted ladder. This shape allows DNA to store enormous amounts of information while remaining compact enough to fit inside the nucleus of a cell.

Each side of the "ladder" is made of repeating molecules called nucleotides. Every nucleotide contains three components:

  • A phosphate group
  • A deoxyribose sugar
  • A nitrogenous base

The four nitrogenous bases found in DNA are:

  • Adenine (A)
  • Thymine (T)
  • Cytosine (C)
  • Guanine (G)

These bases pair according to specific rules known as complementary base pairing:

  • Adenine always pairs with Thymine (A–T)
  • Cytosine always pairs with Guanine (C–G)

The two DNA strands are held together by hydrogen bonds between these base pairs. This predictable pairing allows DNA to copy itself accurately during cell division and helps repair damaged DNA.

The sequence, or order, of the nitrogenous bases forms the genetic code. Even a small change in this sequence can sometimes alter how a protein is produced, potentially affecting how cells function.

How DNA Works

DNA stores information using the specific order of its nitrogenous bases. Cells can read these sequences and use the information to produce proteins, which carry out most of the work inside living organisms. This process is known as gene expression.

1. Transcription

Inside the nucleus, a gene is copied into a temporary molecule called messenger RNA (mRNA). Unlike DNA, mRNA contains the base uracil (U) instead of thymine and can leave the nucleus.

2. Translation

The mRNA travels to a ribosome, where its sequence is read three bases at a time. Each group of three bases is called a codon, and each codon specifies a particular amino acid.

As the ribosome reads the codons, amino acids are joined together in the correct order to form a protein. Once complete, the protein folds into its unique three-dimensional shape and performs its biological function.

Functions

DNA performs many essential functions that allow living organisms to survive and reproduce.

Its primary function is to store genetic information that determines how cells are built and how they function. DNA also provides the instructions needed to produce proteins, which carry out processes such as metabolism, cell signaling, immune defense, and structural support.

DNA is also responsible for:

  • Passing hereditary traits from parents to offspring
  • Directing growth, development, and tissue repair
  • Regulating when certain genes are turned on or off
  • Ensuring genetic information is accurately copied during cell division
  • Providing a source of genetic variation through mutation and recombination

Without DNA, cells would lack the instructions needed to build proteins or coordinate the countless biological processes required for life.

Role in Genetics and Biology

DNA is the foundation of modern genetics because genes are made of DNA. The sequence of nucleotides within each gene determines the instructions used to produce proteins, which in turn influence nearly every characteristic of an organism.

Differences in DNA sequences contribute to the wide variety of traits seen among individuals, such as eye color, blood type, height, and susceptibility to certain diseases. While most DNA sequences are inherited from parents, small changes called mutations can occur naturally or as a result of environmental factors.

Most mutations have little or no effect, but some can alter the structure or function of proteins. These changes may contribute to genetic disorders, increase the risk of disease, or occasionally provide beneficial traits that can be passed to future generations.

Because DNA carries hereditary information, it plays a central role in evolution, inheritance, and the diversity of life on Earth.

Human Relevance

The human genome contains approximately:

  • 3 billion base pairs
  • About 20,000 protein-coding genes
  • 23 pairs of chromosomes

Although humans share more than 99% of their DNA, the small differences between individuals contribute to the unique traits that make each person genetically distinct.

Scientists study DNA to better understand:

  • Genetic disorders
  • Cancer
  • Human evolution and ancestry
  • Personalized medicine
  • Drug development
  • Forensic identification
  • Infectious diseases

Advances in DNA technology have transformed medicine and biological research. Techniques such as DNA sequencing, genetic testing, and CRISPR gene editing allow researchers to identify disease-causing mutations, develop targeted treatments, and explore new approaches to treating inherited disorders.

Interesting Facts

  • DNA was first identified in 1869 by Swiss scientist Friedrich Miescher.
  • The double-helix structure of DNA was described in 1953, based on the work of multiple scientists.
  • Nearly every cell in the human body contains the same DNA, even though different cell types use different genes.
  • More than 99% of human DNA is identical among all people.
  • If stretched end to end, the DNA inside a single human cell would measure about 2 meters (6.5 feet) in length.
  • The DNA in one human cell contains enough information to fill hundreds of large books if written as text.
  • DNA is constantly repaired by specialized enzymes that correct many errors before they become permanent mutations.

Key Takeaway

DNA is the molecule that stores, protects, and passes on the genetic information needed for life. By encoding genes and directing protein synthesis, DNA controls the growth, development, function, and reproduction of living organisms. Its ability to accurately store and transmit biological information makes it one of the most important molecules in all of biology.
